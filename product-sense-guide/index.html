01

Flows & Frictions

Observe the product carefully before forming any opinion.

You are a detective in this step, not a critic. Walk through the product as two different people: a first-time user who just discovered it, and a returning user who uses it regularly. Capture what is smooth and where things break down. Be specific about both.

The core mental model: Always keep Observation and Interpretation in separate columns. Conclusions can be wrong. Observations are evidence.
WHAT I SAWWHAT I THINK IT MEANS
“The app requests notification permissions on the 3rd onboarding screen, before showing the user any value from the product.”“Users who have not yet seen what the app does have no reason to allow notifications, so most leads to will deny access and permanently reduce the app's ability to re-engage them.”

How to do it

  1. Entry / Onboarding
  2. Core Value
  3. Return / Habit
  4. Decision / Commitment

Walk through each as both a first-time and returning user. Capture 3–4 specific moments per flow.

What good looks like

Note what is smooth and what creates friction. Both matter equally. Smooth flows reveal where the product has invested and what users trust.

FLOWOBSERVATIONINTERPRETATIONTYPE
Search → ListingLarge, high-quality photos load before the price is visible. The map and list update seamlessly as the user pans.Visual-first design triggers emotional interest before rational price comparison. Live map sync reduces the mental effort of matching location to listing.SMOOTH
Listing → BookingNightly rate is shown consistently. Total price including service fees appears only at final checkout.Users build a mental price anchor around the lower nightly rate. Late total-price disclosure can create surprise and erode trust.FRICTION
Return VisitWishlists make saving and revisiting easy. No notification appears if a saved listing changes in price or availability.Returning users must manually recheck. This is a missed opportunity for relevant, timely urgency.FRICTION
Common mistakes: Writing conclusions instead of observations; only noting what is broken; trying to cover every screen. Three to four core flows in depth teach far more than ten flows at a surface level.

Output: A completed flow-friction table with 3–4 flows, with observation and interpretation clearly separated.

02

Company Context

Mission, revenue and positioning.

Every product decision exists inside a business context. A product that makes money through advertising builds differently from one that makes money through subscriptions. Understand the mission, how the company earns revenue, and where it is positioned against competitors.

1 · Mission

Find the company's stated mission and write it in your own words. If you cannot paraphrase it in one or two sentences, you have not understood it yet.

2 · Revenue Sources

Investigate who is paying and for what. Identify the revenue sources and which appears primary. If the product looks free, ask who is actually paying.

3 · Competitive Positioning

Pick 2–3 competitors. Explain the specific situation in which a user chooses one over another. Include at least one indirect competitor.

COMPETITOR / ALTERNATIVEWHEN IT WINSWHEN THE PRODUCT WINS
Booking.comUtilitarian transactions, standardized hotel inventory, frictionless price comparison.Experiential travel, unique/localized stays, emotional connection to the destination.
VrboTraditional vacation rentals, entire homes, large families and predictability.Supply diversity, private rooms, unique architecture, urban density and lifestyle brand.
Hotels · indirect competitorPredictability, standardized amenities, business travel, corporate billing, 24/7 front desk.Space-to-price ratio, kitchens/living rooms, neighborhood embeddedness and group-trip economics.
Common mistakes: Copying the mission verbatim; listing features as competitive differences; comparing only direct competitors. Competitive gaps and the revenue model help determine which problems are strategically significant.
03

Feature Reverse-Engineering

Reading features backwards: understand why features were built, not just what they do.

The sequence matters
Part A · How it worksDescribe the feature step by step.
→
Part B · The problem it solvesUser voice first; business voice if you can connect it.
→
Part C · Why it worksPsychology → Behaviour → Business metric.

Select three user-facing features that users interact with directly. Avoid simple utilities such as a search bar or back button.

PARTQUESTION
How it worksWhat does the user see? What can they do? What happens as a result? Capture 3–5 clear mechanics.
The problem it solvesWrite the user's frustration in first person. Stretch: connect the feature to what the business is losing without it.
Why it worksWhat specific emotion or psychology is engaged? What behaviour follows? What business metric moves?

Full example · “Contact Host”

How it works: A Contact Host button appears before booking; users type a question; the host receives it with listing context; both parties see the thread in-app; the thread continues after booking.

User problem: “Before I commit and hand over money, I want to confirm a few specific things — is the WiFi reliable for video calls? Can I check in at 3pm? I feel nervous booking from a stranger without being able to ask first.”

Business problem (stretch): Uncertain guests abandon listing pages without booking. Reviews and photos may not sufficiently reduce anxiety for high-consideration stays.

Why it works: Psychology: reduces perceived risk by making the host feel human. Behaviour: conversation creates soft commitment through invested effort. Metric: booking conversion rate.

Common mistakes: Picking features that are too simple; starting with the problem before describing how the feature works; stopping at “it improves engagement.” Engagement is a correlation — trace the actual mechanism.
04

User Analysis & Segmentation

Identify who the product actually serves and what each group needs.

Two people using the same product can have completely different goals, contexts, relationships and frustrations. Segment users by meaningful use cases so product decisions can be specific rather than vaguely trying to serve everyone.

Mutually Exclusive

A user can clearly belong to only one primary segment. If the same person fits two equally well, sharpen or merge.

Collectively Exhaustive

Segments account for all meaningful types of users. If a significant group fits nowhere, a segment is missing.

Actionable

You could build the product differently for each segment. If decisions would be identical, the segments are not meaningfully distinct.

How to approach it: list real use cases from the full product, pricing, features and company description. Cluster similar goals and contexts into primary segments; find meaningful differences within them as sub-segments.

PRODUCT TYPESTARTING PROMPTS
MarketplaceWho is on each side of the transaction? What brings supply-side vs demand-side users here?
Content & MediaActive seeking vs passive consumption? What different contexts do people watch/listen in?
Social PlatformsCreating/sharing vs consuming? What relationship is being maintained or built?
Productivity & ToolsPersonal vs collaborative goals? How embedded is the product in daily workflow?
Learning & EducationDaily habit, job/exam outcome, or external pressure?
Fintech & PaymentsOccasional transactions or most financial activity? What financial jobs are being done?
When a problem affects multiple segments: ask which segment experiences it most frequently, most painfully and most consequentially. You cannot interview everyone; you need a specific person to listen for.

01

User Research

Interviews, survey and research debrief.

The right mindset: “I want to understand how people actually behave.” Not “I need to prove my hypotheses are correct.” Some hypotheses being wrong is a valuable outcome.

The research process has two parts: gather raw data through user interviews and a survey; then synthesize it into actionable insights.

Minimum research

5 user interviews and a target of 25+ survey respondents.

Research readout

Do not submit a chaotic dump of notes. Synthesize findings into: Objective & Methodology → Insights → Hypothesis Check.

SECTIONEXAMPLE
Research Objective & MethodologyObjective: understand how active music listeners consume music, whether algorithmic discovery creates fatigue, and core frictions in how the product maps user taste; remain open to other problem areas.
Method: 5 interviews with active premium discovery users; 28 discovery-driven survey respondents; secondary analysis of 150+ synthesized Reddit, App Store and community sentiments.
Insights from UsersInsight 1 — Discovery feels transactional, not emotional. Qual: “I made a playlist of my 40 favorite songs because I read on Reddit it helps the algorithm. I honestly cannot tell if it worked. I spend hours trying to ‘train’ it.” Observed workaround: users invest manual time creating taste playlists to proactively provide data to a system they feel ignores their actual preferences.
Insight 2 — Spotify is the destination for playing, not finding music. Qual: “Someone uses a song in a TikTok video, I Shazam it, find it on Spotify. That happens two or three times a week. I'm finding music for Spotify, not on Spotify.” Quant: 72% selected social media (TikTok/Reels) as the source of the last song they loved, beating Discover Weekly by a 4-to-1 margin.
Hypothesis check

VALIDATED

  1. Discovery causes fatigue — confirmed.
  2. Taste bubbles create a trap — confirmed; 78% validated repetition.

INVALIDATED

  1. Users switch apps — false; users tend to give up on discovery and return to their own library.
  2. Navigation is confusing — false; UI navigation is intuitive, but content remains insufficiently exploratory.

DISCOVERED NEW

  1. “Quarantine” need: prevent utility audio from skewing taste.
  2. Feedback-loop paranoia: users who like but never dislike fear the algorithm cannot learn.
  3. Algorithmic manipulation: burner accounts or muted playlists are used to “train” the algorithm.
Common mistakes: Asking leading questions; ignoring contradictory signals; treating the research as proof of what you already believe. The goal is truth, not confirmation.
02

Competitive Check

Sharpen your focus around each validated problem.

For each validated problem, examine one or two direct competitors. Ask: Have they addressed it, and how? Then ask what their approach — or absence of one — tells you about trade-offs, difficulty and opportunity.

VALIDATED PROBLEMCOMPETITORWHAT THEY HAVE DONEWHAT IT TELLS YOU
Discover Weekly feels impersonal; algorithm does not explain why songs appear.Apple MusicSimilar algorithm-driven playlists such as New Music Mix, with no explanation of why tracks are recommended.No direct streaming competitor has solved algorithmic transparency well. This remains an open industry problem.
Users discover music on TikTok first, then come to Spotify to play it.YouTube MusicDeep integration with YouTube videos, live sessions and creator-shared audio.There is a structural social-discovery advantage. “Spotify is the destination, not the starting point” is a competitive vulnerability, not merely a user preference.
If a competitor has already solved a problem well: that tells you what a working solution looks like and what trade-offs were made. You can still solve it differently or better.
03

Goal Setting

Clustering problems and finding direction.

Let evidence lead to a goal — not the other way around. Group each validated problem by the business metric it most directly affects, then choose the cluster with the strongest evidence and strongest business connection.

METRICWHAT IT MEASURESPOINT PROBLEMS HERE WHEN…
AcquisitionNew users discovering and joining.Users cannot find the product or understand its value before trying it.
ActivationNew users experiencing real value for the first time.New users drop off before the moment that would make them want to stay.
EngagementHow often and how deeply existing users interact.Sessions shorten, visits become less frequent or core features go underused.
RetentionUsers continuing over time.Users stop subscribing, uninstall or replace the product.
MonetisationRevenue generated from users.Users do not convert, downgrade or question value for money.
ReferralUsers bringing in other users.Users would not recommend the product or discourage others.
Goal statement: “Improve [business metric] for [user segment].” Add a timeframe or directional target only when your research supports it.

Spotify problem clustering example

PROBLEMMETRICWHY IT MATTERS

Why the Engagement cluster? Three validated problems point to the same surface, confirmed by 4 of 5 interviewees and a 61% survey majority. Weekly engagement affects both ad impressions on the free tier and perceived value of the premium subscription.

Goal: Improve engagement for discovery-driven listeners.

Common mistakes: Picking a goal that sounds impressive but is not grounded; using vague goals such as “improve user experience”; choosing the metric before looking at problems.
04

User Personas

Bring the user to life for decision-making.

A persona is not a marketing profile. It is a decision-making tool. Build one or two personas for the segment most affected by your goal cluster. Ground them in what you heard: quotes, behaviours, workarounds and evidence. Inferences are valid when they are based on patterns.

ALEX
CHEN

Alex Chen

Relationship: Discovery-driven premium listener who wants Spotify to understand his taste without making discovery feel like work.

Goals & motivations

  • Discover artists before they go mainstream.
  • Find the right music for work, commute, gym and winding down.
  • Have Spotify understand his taste well enough that discovery feels effortless.

Behaviours

  • Opens Spotify 4–5 times a week; more on Mondays.
  • Rarely saves songs; usually likes and moves on.
  • Discovers music on TikTok or from friends, then uses Spotify to listen.
  • Skips Discover Weekly after 3–4 generic songs and returns to Daily Mix.

Frustrations

  • “Discover Weekly started replaying the same five artists. It stopped surprising me.”
  • “I don't understand why it doesn't get better. I use it every day.”

Context

Premium subscriber; phone primary, desktop while working; does not use Spotify for podcasts.

“Spotify knows what I have played. It doesn't know what I actually felt about it.”

Relevance check: Yes. Alex's weekly routine directly exposes algorithm quality, the missing negative feedback signal, and the fact that discovery starts outside Spotify. All three Engagement-cluster problems show up in his week.

01

Brainstorm Solutions

Diverge before you converge.

Start from the validated problem. Break it into root causes and brainstorm against each root cause. Push past the obvious; focus on impact, not novelty.

Example problem: “Users feel frustrated by unexpected hidden fees during checkout on Airbnb, leading to cart abandonment and booking drop-off.” Root causes: lack of fee transparency upfront, fee variations across listings, confusing final price structure.
TECHNIQUEWHAT IT ISHOW TO USE ITWHY IT WORKS
How Might WeReframes problems as open challenges.Turn pain points into “How might we…?” statements, e.g. “How might we help guests trust new hosts faster?”Shifts mindset from criticism to creation.
Journey MappingVisualizes every user step.Map the end-to-end journey, mark painful moments, ideate one solution per pain point.Ensures ideas solve real experience gaps.
Analogy ThinkingDraws from other domains.Ask how other products or industries solve a similar challenge.Learn from proven mental models.
Root-Cause & Issue TreeBreaks a problem into smaller causes.Ask “Why?” repeatedly, reach underlying causes, solve each.Creates leverage by fixing problems at the root.
SCAMPERSystematically transforms ideas.Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify, Put to another use, Eliminate, Reverse.Encourages structured creativity.
Crazy 8sRapid sketching.Fold a sheet into 8 sections and sketch one idea per section in 8 minutes.Pushes beyond the first safe idea.
Constraint ReversalFlips constraints.Ask “What if we had unlimited budget/time?” or “What if we could only change one button?”Reveals moonshots and hyper-focused quick wins.

Aim for 10–15 ideas. Do not judge or rank yet. Sort them into three buckets:

HIGH CONFIDENCE

Fairly certain based on user insights, competitive examples or logical reasoning.

LOW CONFIDENCE

Could work, but needs more validation or clarity before committing.

MOONSHOT

Big, bold, unconventional and potentially game-changing if feasible.

Common mistakes: Jumping to UI tweaks; brainstorming in isolation from validated insights; writing vague ideas such as “Improve experience” or “Add AI recommendations.” If stuck, look at competitors and analogous products, use HMW framing, mix realistic and ambitious ideas, and state the one key assumption for each idea.

03

Build a Live Prototype

Make your solution real.

A live, clickable prototype creates reactions that a static diagram never will. It surfaces problems early and lets designers and engineers react to something concrete.

Sketch the flow first · 15 minutes
1 · Entry pointWhere is the user coming from?
2 · ChangeWhat is different from the current product?
3 · User actionWhat does the user do to experience the change?
4 · Moment of valueWhat can they do or decide better?

Google AI Studio

Browser-based, no setup. Build from plain language. Suitable for absolute first-timers.

Lovable

Beginner-friendly browser tool producing polished React apps. Structured planning helps prevent common mistakes.

Bolt / Claude Code

Bolt is browser-based and fast; Claude Code is terminal-based and better suited to technical users comfortable reviewing code.

The evolving PM role: Product managers increasingly express ideas interactively. A working prototype can align teams early, gather feedback faster and demonstrate clarity of thought.
04

Plan Measurement

Define success metrics.

Start from the goal. Identify the solution. Choose one key metric, then secondary metrics that explain movement and guardrails that prevent unintended harm.

GoalIncrease booking conversions for new or unrated listings.
SolutionIntroduce a Trust Badge to increase confidence.
Key metricThe single metric that tells you whether the solution worked.
Supporting systemSecondary metrics explain movement; guardrails protect users and business.
METRIC TYPEWHAT IT ISWHY IT MATTERS
KEYThe single metric that tells you whether the solution worked.Direct answer to the goal.
SECONDARYMetrics that explain why the key metric is moving; e.g. clicks, messages, time spent.Diagnose the mechanism.
GUARDRAILMetrics that ensure the solution does not harm users or the business; e.g. cancellations, host visibility, complaints.Protect against unintended consequences.
Product sense test: If you cannot explain what should move, why it should move, and how you will know, you have not finished the product thinking.
L180 Playbook

How to practice this as a team

Use the guide as a repeatable thinking discipline, not as a document to memorize.

01Pick a product you actually use and have opinions about.
02Observe before interpreting. Keep evidence and hypotheses separate.
03Understand the business model before declaring a “good” or “bad” product decision.
04Reverse-engineer meaningful features: mechanics → user problem → psychology → behaviour → metric.
05Segment by use case and context, not by superficial demographics.
06Interview to learn behaviour, not to get people to agree with your idea.
07Let evidence determine the goal and the user segment you focus on.
08Generate broadly before prioritizing. Do not confuse the first idea with the best idea.
09Prioritize using explicit assumptions and trade-offs.
10Prototype the logic before polishing screens.
11Every solution should trace back to a validated problem.
12Every success metric should trace back to the goal.
The one-line mental model

Observe → Understand the business → Reverse-engineer → Segment → Research → Validate → Cluster → Set a goal → Persona → Diverge → Prioritize → Prototype → Measure.

The biggest product-sense trap

Jumping from a vague problem to a feature. Strong product thinking inserts evidence, mechanism, trade-offs and measurable outcomes between the two.
